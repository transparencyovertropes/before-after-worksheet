Before-and-After Transparency Worksheet

When someone says authority or money is “moving,” this worksheet asks the two questions that usually go unanswered: authority over what, exactly? and what happens to the dollars?

Fill it in for any change you are trying to understand. It does not tell you whether a change is good or bad. It just makes the change visible, side by side, so people can see it and talk about it.

Your answers stay on your device. Nothing you type is sent anywhere. There is no tracking and no account. If you use the Save button, your work is stored in this browser only. Clearing your browser data will erase it, so print or export anything you want to keep.

How to use this worksheet

  1. Pick one change to study. One program, one policy, one budget line. Do not try to cover everything at once.
  2. Fill in Part 1 — who made the decisions before, and who makes them after.
  3. Fill in Part 2 — what the dollars were, and what they will be.
  4. Read Part 3. It adds up your answers for you.
  5. Answer Part 4 in plain words, including the honest list of what nobody knows yet.
  6. Write down your sources. Every claim needs a “where I learned this.” That is what makes it transparency instead of opinion.

One rule that keeps this honest

If you cannot name a source for a “before” or an “after,” do not guess. Choose Not yet decided or unclear. A worksheet full of honest blanks is far more useful — and far more credible — than one full of confident guesses.

Part 1. Who makes the decisions?

“Authority” is not one thing. It is several different jobs, and they can move in different directions at the same time. Break the decision apart before you judge it.

The four jobs hiding inside the word “authority”

Researchers who study who-decides-what have found that authority breaks into four jobs. Someone has to set the direction. Someone has to build the organization that will do the work. Someone has to run and check the work. And someone has to actually do it. This worksheet uses those four jobs, rewritten for public services like schools and health care.

  1. A. Setting the overall direction — what are we trying to do, and who is it for?
    • Who decides the goals, and what counts as success?
    • Who decides who is covered and who is eligible?
    • Who decides what is included and what is left out?
  2. B. Building the organization and its supports — who is set up to do the work?
    • Who decides which agency or office runs it?
    • Who decides on staffing, and who can move or remove people?
    • Who decides what supports exist — data systems, training, help lines, ways to file a complaint?
  3. C. Running the work and checking it — how is it done, and who watches?
    • Who writes and changes the rules and procedures?
    • Who tracks progress?
    • Who checks quality and enforces the rules?
    • Who decides what gets reported to the public?
  4. D. Doing the work — who carries it out, day to day?
    • Who designs the actual programs and services?
    • Who delivers them to students, patients, and families?
    • Who decides how the money gets spent day to day?

Adapted from Hackman’s authority matrix and from Moe and colleagues’ (2021) study of team autonomy, which used those same four areas to sort out who really held authority. Full citations are at the end of this worksheet.

These four jobs come apart in practice. One office can keep job A while a different office takes over job D. On paper that can look like “nothing changed.” In daily life it can change a great deal.

The four jobs stack. They are not a flat list — they build on each other. Whoever sets the direction (A) puts limits on whoever builds the organization (B), who puts limits on whoever runs the work (C), who puts limits on whoever does it (D).

That is why the order matters. Gaining job D and job C is real, and it is not the same as gaining job A. A group can be handed a great deal of day-to-day freedom while someone else still decides the goals, who is covered, and what counts as success. Both of those things can be described as “more authority.” They are not the same amount.

Four rungs on the ladder

Counting up from the bottom, how far a group has climbed has a name:

  • Carrying out only — the group does the work (D). Everything else is decided elsewhere.
  • Self-managing — the group does the work and runs and checks it (D and C).
  • Self-designing — the group also shapes the organization and its supports (D, C, and B).
  • Self-governing — the group also sets the direction (all four).

Part 3 works out these rungs for you, before and after, from what you enter in the table below. If the answers do not stack neatly — say, a group sets the direction but does not run the work — the worksheet will say so rather than force it onto a rung.

Where does money fit?

Money runs through all four jobs. How much and for what purpose is direction (A). Who processes it is organization (B). What it can be spent on is rules (C). How it is spent day to day is doing the work (D).

So keep the dollar amounts in Part 2 and the control question here in Part 1. When you find that an amount held steady but the strings loosened, that is a change in job A or C showing up in Part 2 — worth naming in both places.

Watch for three directions, not two. A decision can move down (federal to state to local), up (local or state to federal), or sideways (from one federal office to a different federal office). A sideways move is still a real change — but it is not the same as sending something to the states, and the two often get described with the same words.

Part 2. What happens to the money?

Dollars are only half the story. A funding line can stay at the same number while the rules attached to it change completely — and that changes who really controls it. Track both.

Two questions for every dollar

1. How much? The amount before and the amount after.

2. How tightly is it tied? Money can arrive with a narrow required purpose, with some flexibility, or as a flexible pot the receiver can spend across many purposes. Loosening the strings is a real transfer of control even when the dollar amount does not move at all.

There is no overall score here, on purpose. It would be easy to multiply the weights by the distance each decision moved and produce one number. That number would look authoritative and would hide everything that matters: which jobs moved, which direction, who now decides, and what is still unknown.

Collapsing a change into a single figure is the very thing this worksheet exists to undo. Report the rows.

Part 4. What people should know

Help with the words

Plain-language glossary
Formula funding
Money handed out by a set math formula — usually based on things like how many students a place has. Nobody competes for it; the formula decides.
Categorical grant
Money that must be spent on one specific purpose. Narrow strings.
Block grant
Several funding streams combined into one pot with fewer required uses. The receiver gets more choice about how to spend it. Supporters call this flexibility; critics say it makes spending harder to track and compare.
Waiver
Official permission to skip a rule that would normally apply. Waivers are usually requested by a state and approved case by case, so two states can end up under different rules.
Transferability
Permission to move money from one program into another program. The total does not change; where it lands does.
Interagency agreement
A written deal in which one federal agency has another federal agency carry out some of its work. It moves the work sideways within the federal government. It does not, by itself, change what the law requires.
Statutory authority
Power that Congress wrote into law. An agency generally cannot hand it away on its own — changing it takes an act of Congress. This is why some changes are described as temporary or as administrative even when they feel permanent.
Self-managing, self-designing, self-governing
Names for how much authority a group holds, from the research this worksheet borrows from. Self-managing means you do the work and run it. Self-designing means you also shape the organization around it. Self-governing means you also set the goals. Each rung includes the ones below it.
Guardrails
The conditions, reporting rules, and protections attached to money. Ask who was protected by a guardrail before you decide whether removing it is a gain or a loss.
A filled-in example

Here is one real change, filled in the way this worksheet asks. It is a useful example precisely because it shows a sideways move that is easy to describe wrongly.

The change: In June 2026, the U.S. Department of Education announced agreements to have other federal agencies help carry out special education program administration and education civil rights enforcement.

A. Setting the overall directionBefore: Congress and the U.S. Dept. of Education → After: unchanged. No change. The underlying law was written by Congress, and an agreement between two agencies does not rewrite it.
B. Building the organizationBefore: U.S. Dept. of Education → After: shared with a different federal agency. Moved sideways. This is the row that actually moved: which office is staffed and set up to run the work.
C. Running the work and checking itBefore: U.S. Dept. of Education → After: shared with a different federal agency for monitoring and compliance. Moved sideways.
D. Doing the workGrant processing and fund drawdowns → a different federal agency. Moved sideways.
DollarsLeave blank until you can cite a published figure. The agreements shift who administers funds; that is a different question from whether the amount changed.
Still unknownCourt cases were pending and separate bills had been introduced that would move authority permanently rather than administratively. Both could change the answer.

On the ladder, the states climbed no rungs at all here — every move was sideways between federal offices. Notice what the worksheet caught: nothing here moved to the states, even though this change is often discussed under the heading of returning education to the states. Some other changes in the same period — waivers and combined funding streams — did move choices to states. They are different changes and deserve separate rows.

Verify the details and dates yourself before you publish anything based on this example. It is here to show the method, not to serve as your source.

Staying fair when you share this publicly
  • Separate the facts from your reading of them. Parts 1 and 2 are for what is documented. Part 4 is where interpretation belongs.
  • Date everything. Write the date you filled it in, right on the page. A worksheet from three months ago may describe a world that no longer exists.
  • Say what you left out. If you only studied one program out of many, say so plainly at the top.
  • Use neutral verbs. “Moved,” “changed,” “now handled by.” Save the stronger words for a place where you have clearly labeled them as your view.
  • Invite correction. Publish a way for people to tell you that you got something wrong, and fix it visibly when they do.

Where the framework comes from

The four decision areas in Part 1 are adapted from research on how authority gets divided inside organizations. In the original work the unit was a team inside a company, and the questions were about products and staffing. Here the unit is whoever runs a public service — a federal office, a state agency, a school district — and the questions are about programs, eligibility, and rules. The structure is theirs; the translation to public services, and any errors in it, are not.

Hackman set out four functions that must be filled whenever work gets done in an organization, and showed that a unit’s authority can be described by how many of them it holds. Moe and colleagues used those four areas as the backbone of a study of who actually decided what across two large organizations — which is the closest model for what this worksheet does. Kozlowski and Ilgen’s review places the work in the wider research on groups and teams.

  • Hackman, J. R. (1986). The psychology of self-management in organizations. In M. S. Pallak & R. O. Perloff (Eds.), Psychology and work: Productivity, change, and employment (pp. 89–136). American Psychological Association.
  • Kozlowski, S. W. J., & Ilgen, D. R. (2006). Enhancing the effectiveness of work groups and teams. Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 7(3), 77–124. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1529-1006.2006.00030.x (opens in a new tab)
  • Moe, N. B., Šmite, D., Paasivaara, M., & Lassenius, C. (2021). Finding the sweet spot for organizational control and team autonomy in large-scale agile software development.
